# Locales

How each market reads store copy, so a locale gets its own words, not a translation. Read the market's entry before writing or reviewing any headline or label in it. Two markets that share a language are two markets: copy written for the neighbour reads as native and is wrong, and nothing in the editor can see it.

A locale not listed here has no market reference. Tell the user, write from general knowledge, and report that copy as unchecked.

## Rules for every market

- **Rewrite for the market**, from the source idea. A line that feels forced in English is not rescued by translation.
- **Type sentence case** and let the deck set capitals: Scene > Headline > UPPER applies the market's own casing rules (ß becomes SS, accents stay on capitals). Capitalising every word is an English habit and marks copy as translated on sight.
- **Hold one form of address** across the whole deck. A deck that slides between two forms sounds like two writers.
- **Fit the box by rewriting.** Shrinking the type to make a long line fit trades a visible problem for an unreadable one; cut a word instead. German, Dutch and Portuguese run longest.
- **Report judgement, never decide it.** Whether a line sounds native is the user's call. Hand back every doubt with the copy.

## English (`en`)

International store audience, not one country. Address: direct "you". Sentence case for body copy; Title Case on a headline is a choice held across the deck. Product names keep their owners' casing (iPhone, iOS, macOS).
Avoid: regionalisms that name a country ("queue", "gotten"), sports metaphors, startup register (leverage, unlock, supercharge, game-changing).

## German (`de`)

Germany. Address: "Sie", never mixed with "du", capitalised everywhere (Sie, Ihnen, Ihr). Register: one notch more sober; a Playful line loses its exclamation marks, warmth comes from precision, and an overstated claim reads as untrustworthy.
Caps: nouns always take a capital (spelling, not style); everything else sentence case. Umlauts stay Ä, Ö, Ü in capitals, never AE, OE, UE. ß becomes SS in capitals.
Avoid: Lederhosen, beer steins, Oktoberfest, efficiency jokes, blackletter type, superlatives ("revolutionär").

## Spanish (`es`)

Spain, readable across Latin America. Address: "tú", usually carried by the verb; "usted" reads as a bank letter, "vosotros" belongs to Spain alone. Register: a little more expressive; length is not padding.
Caps: sentence case, days, months, languages and nationalities lowercase. Accents stay on capitals; Ñ is its own letter. Write the opening ¿ and ¡.
Avoid: Latin American vocabulary as if one market (celular, computadora, platicar), flamenco and bullfighting, siesta and mañana jokes, regional symbols (Catalan, Basque, Galician).

## French (`fr`)

France. Address: "vous" throughout; "tu" claims a familiarity the brand has not earned. The imperative carries most lines. Register: one notch more composed; wit through precise wording, not exclamation marks.
Caps: sentence case. Accents stay on capitals (É, À, Ç). A narrow non-breaking space precedes : ; ! ? and guillemets are the quotation marks.
Avoid: berets, baguettes, the Eiffel Tower as stand-in for the country, anglicisms where French has its own word, perfume-advert luxury framing.

## Italian (`it`)

Italy. Address: "tu", usually carried by the verb; "Lei" reads as a utility writing to a customer. Register: warmer, a longer and more musical sentence is not padding.
Caps: sentence case. È, À, Ù keep the accent in capitals; "E'" is a typewriter habit. Elisions are tight: l'app, un'idea.
Avoid: pizza, pasta, gondolas, mafia references, hand-gesture jokes, football clubs and colours, Spanish material borrowed for the Mediterranean.

## Dutch (`nl`)

Netherlands. Address: "je", held throughout; "u" only for copy about money or law, and then for the whole deck. Register: plainer and more direct; understatement is trusted, superlatives are discounted.
Caps: sentence case. IJ is one letter, both rise together (IJsland). Articles and particles keep their lowercase (de Jong).
Avoid: tulips, clogs, windmills, cheese, Amsterdam's coffee-shop reputation, "Holland" for the Netherlands, Belgian vocabulary as if one market (gsm, schoon), hard-sell enthusiasm.

## Brazilian Portuguese (`pt-BR`)

Brazil. Address: "você", written openly and often; dropping the pronoun reads as imported. Register: one notch warmer and more direct; exclamation marks read as energy.
Caps: sentence case; days, months and languages lowercase. Accents stay on capitals. Brazilian spelling throughout: fatos, ação, time, tela (not factos, acção, equipa, ecrã).
Avoid: European Portuguese material (calçada portuguesa, azulejos, Manueline stonework), carnival, football shirts and samba as shorthand, Amazon as stand-in for the country, favela imagery.

## European Portuguese (`pt-PT`)

Portugal. Address: "você" or, more often, no pronoun at all ("Guarde tudo num só sítio"). "Tu" is a friend speaking. Matching the Brazilian cadence is the clearest way to sound foreign. Register: one notch more courteous; a Playful line drops its exclamation marks.
Caps: sentence case; days, months and languages lowercase. Accents stay on capitals.
Avoid: Brazilian references (Copacabana, tropical planting, Niemeyer curves), flamenco and bullfighting, football clubs, fado and saudade as a product mood.
